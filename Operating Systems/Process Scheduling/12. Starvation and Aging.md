# Starvation and Aging

**Starvation** and **Aging** are closely related concepts in **CPU scheduling**.  

They deal with the **fairness of resource allocation** and ensure that **every process eventually gets a chance to execute**.

---

## 1. Starvation

### Definition
**Starvation** (also called *indefinite postponement*) occurs when:

> A process waits for CPU or other resources **for an unbounded amount of time** because other processes are continuously favored.

The process is ready but **never selected by the scheduler**.

---

## 2. Causes of Starvation

Starvation typically occurs in scheduling algorithms that favor certain processes:

### 2.1 Priority Scheduling
- High-priority processes always get CPU
- Low-priority processes may never run

### 2.2 Shortest Job First (SJF / SRTF)
- Short jobs keep executing
- Long jobs keep waiting

### 2.3 Multilevel Queue Scheduling
- Fixed priority queues
- Lower queues may never get CPU time

---

## 3. Example of Starvation

### Priority Scheduling Example

| Process | Priority | Burst Time |
|-------|----------|------------|
| P1 | High | 4 |
| P2 | Low | 10 |

If high-priority processes keep arriving:
- P2 stays in ready queue forever
- P2 is starved

---

## 4. Effects of Starvation

- Poor system fairness
- Unpredictable execution
- Some processes may never complete
- Bad user experience

---

## 5. Aging

### Definition
**Aging** is a technique used to **prevent starvation** by:

> Gradually increasing the priority of a process the longer it waits in the system.

This ensures that **even low-priority processes eventually get CPU time**.

---

## 6. How Aging Works

- Each process starts with an initial priority
- While waiting in the ready queue:
  - its priority is increased periodically
- Eventually, it becomes high enough to be scheduled

---

## 7. Aging Example

### Without Aging
```

Low-priority process → waits indefinitely

```

### With Aging
```

Priority increases over time → process gets CPU

```

#### Example Policy
```

Every 5 seconds waiting → increase priority by 1

```

---

## 8. Aging in Different Scheduling Algorithms

### Priority Scheduling
- Aging is most commonly applied
- Prevents low-priority starvation

### SJF / SRTF
- Aging can increase effective priority of long jobs

### Multilevel Feedback Queue (MLFQ)
- Priority boosting is a form of aging

---

## 9. Starvation vs Deadlock

| Aspect | Starvation | Deadlock |
|------|------------|----------|
| Cause | Scheduling unfairness | Circular resource wait |
| CPU availability | CPU is free but not allocated | CPU may be idle |
| Resolution | Aging, fair scheduling | Deadlock prevention/avoidance |
| Scope | Scheduling issue | Resource allocation issue |

---

## 10. Advantages vs Disadvantages of Aging

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------------|---------------|
| Prevents starvation | Increases scheduling complexity |
| Ensures fairness | May lead to priority inversion |
| Simple to implement | Requires tuning of aging parameters |
| Works with various algorithms | May affect overall performance |

---

## Summary

| Concept | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Starvation | Indefinite waiting |
| Cause | Unfair scheduling |
| Aging | Priority increase over time |
| Purpose | Ensure fairness |

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Starvation** occurs when a process never gets CPU  

- Common in priority-based and shortest-job scheduling  

- **Aging** is the primary solution to starvation  

- Aging ensures fairness without removing priorities  
- Widely used in modern schedulers
